## Narration

### Hook
> Binance's first explanation was one engineer's laptop.
> Then Binance published its own report.
> According to that report, the attack did not start on October twenty-ninth. It started in June, when someone searched for an AI coding tool.
> In the next few minutes: the timeline Binance says the hackers followed, who the FBI links to it, and why the money "coming back" is not what it sounds like.

### One idea
> First, one idea, because it explains everything that follows.
> Hackers do not always need to break the locks. Sometimes they just need to reach the people who hold the keys.
> A software engineer's computer is not just a computer. It is a door into the systems that move money.
> So the real question is not how the hackers got in on October twenty-ninth. It is how long they had already been inside.

### June
> Let's start where Binance says it started. June.
> According to Binance's report, as reported by CNBC and CoinDesk, a Binance wallet engineer went online looking for a popular AI coding tool.
> They searched for it, the way millions of developers do.
> What they downloaded was not the real thing. It was a tampered version of a developer installer called Homebrew.
> Nothing about that looks like an attack. It looks like a normal workday.
> This is not a story about one careless person. It is about a trap that works on careful people.
> And according to Binance, that was the hackers' first foothold.

### September
> Next, September.
> Binance says the attackers moved on to people, using fake recruiters and fake job interviews.
> A fake job offer is a perfect cover. A stranger offers something people want, and asks them to do something on their own computer.
> According to Binance, an employee was lured in by a fake recruiter.
> According to Binance, someone posing as a project director contacted a software engineer through a job platform and set up two interviews.
>
> And those interviews, as Binance tells it, became the bridge to everything that came next.

### Nine days before
> Now jump to nine days before the hack.
> Binance says the attackers used the laptop of the employee who had been lured by the fake interview. From there, they got into the computers of two more software engineers who worked on Binance's wallets.
> That is the moment this stops being a one-laptop story.
> Then, according to the report, they compromised Binance's own internal AI tools.
> And through those tools, they reached the system that approves digital currency transactions.
> Think about what that means. The hackers were no longer outside, trying to break in. They were inside, close to the people who approve payments.
> That is why Binance's co-CEO, Richard Teng, said the attackers, quote, "spent months building their way in."
> Binance also said this was not one mistake or one breach.

### October 29
> Then came October twenty-ninth.
> Everything so far was preparation. This is the moment the money moved.
> According to Binance, the attackers used a new Ethereum feature. Coverage identifies it as EIP seven-seven-zero-two.
> In plain terms, the feature lets a wallet sign a permission that lets a program act on its behalf. Think of it as a power of attorney for a crypto wallet.
> The security firm Blockaid reportedly found that the attacker bundled fifty of these signed permissions into a single transaction.
> Binance says this let the attackers take control of a hot wallet, a wallet connected to the internet that holds money for withdrawals, and move funds outside the normal withdrawal process.
>
> To be clear, the report does not say Ethereum itself was hacked. According to Binance, the attackers abused a legitimate feature, through access to Binance's own signing process.
> The result was the largest crypto theft on record. Binance puts the affected assets at about six point three four billion dollars.

### The twist
> Here is the twist.
> Binance paused withdrawals, and that should have stopped it.
> According to Teng, it did not. The feature the attackers used kept funds moving even after the pause.
> In other words, the lock Binance reached for was not the door the money was leaving through.

### The money
> So what about the money?
> You may have seen that Binance says it has restored about ninety-eight percent of the affected assets. That sounds like almost everything came back.
> It did not.
> Restored means Binance replaced the assets with its own money. According to Teng, it also set aside more than the full amount required.
> That is different from getting the money back from the hackers.
> About two point eight five billion dollars is frozen, according to Teng. Other reports say about three billion. Frozen means it cannot move. It does not mean it is back.
> As of November seventh, no seizure of the stolen funds from the attackers has been announced.

### Who
> So who did it?
> Binance has not named anyone itself. Teng acknowledged that the FBI has linked the attack to North Korean hackers, a group called TraderTraitor.
> The blockchain analytics firms TRM Labs and Chainalysis reached the same conclusion earlier.
> North Korea has rejected the allegations.
> This would not be the first time. The FBI attributed the twenty twenty-five Bybit heist, about one and a half billion dollars, to TraderTraitor as well.
> And not everyone thinks Binance's account ends the story. The founder of another exchange, CoinEx's Haipo Yang, argues the hack could have been prevented. That is his opinion, not a finding.

### What we don't know
> So what don't we know?
> Binance has not published technical details of the malware.
> We have Binance's account of how it happened, and it has not been independently verified.
> We don't know how much of the frozen money will ever be recovered, or whether anyone will be charged.

### Close
> Here is the part I keep coming back to.
> The biggest crypto heist in history did not begin with a clever break-in on October twenty-ninth. According to Binance, it began in June, with an ordinary search.
> If the frozen money starts to move, or Binance publishes more, I will update this video.
> If you want to follow this story as it develops, subscribe.
