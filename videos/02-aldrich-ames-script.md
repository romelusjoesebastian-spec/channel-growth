## 1. Hook (0:00-0:30)

Visual: dark office corridor, then a stack of files, then a close-up of an empty desk. Cut every 3-5 seconds. No logo, no intro.
On-screen: "1985-1994"

> For nine years, the CIA kept losing its best spies inside the Soviet Union. They were arrested. At least ten of them died. And the man who had sold them out was working inside the CIA, spending more than six hundred thousand dollars on a house and cars no government salary could pay for. Nobody stopped him. Here is how he got away with it, and the small team that finally caught him.

## 2. Who he was

Visual: stylized silhouette of a man in a suit at a desk; Langley-style office building exterior (illustration, not a real photo).
On-screen: "Aldrich Ames. CIA officer, 31 years."

> His name was Aldrich Ames. He was not an outsider who slipped past security. By the time he was arrested, he was a thirty-one-year veteran of the Central Intelligence Agency. He had worked in the part of the CIA that handled the Soviet Union, and that meant he had access to exactly the kind of information Moscow wanted most. All the Soviets needed was someone inside who was willing to sell it.

## 3. April 1985

Visual: calendar page flipping to April 1985; a sealed envelope; a handoff in a parking lot (illustration).
On-screen: "April 1985: the espionage begins" then "100+ operations compromised" then "~$2.5 million"

> In April 1985, he made his choice. Ames began spying for the Soviet Union. And he did not do it once. He kept doing it for almost nine years. The FBI says what he passed along led to the compromise of more than one hundred American intelligence operations. And the Senate Intelligence Committee later estimated that he may have been paid around two and a half million dollars for it.
>
> But the money is not the part that matters. The part that matters is who paid the price.

## 4. The people who disappeared

Visual: slow pacing. A row of empty chairs; a map of the Soviet Union with markers going dark one by one; silhouettes fading. Quieter music.
On-screen: "At least 10 died"

> Think about who these people were. They were inside the Soviet system, quietly helping the United States. They had taken risks most of us cannot picture. Some had been trusted for years.
>
> Then, one by one, they were gone. Arrested. Silenced. At least ten of them died.
>
> And inside the CIA, the people who had worked with them struggled to explain it. Operations that had worked for years suddenly collapsed. Every possible explanation was a bad one. Either the Soviets had found a way to break the CIA's methods from the outside, or someone on the inside was handing them over.
>
> That second possibility was the one nobody wanted to say out loud.

## 5. The mole hunt

Visual: a small room, a table, stacks of case files, a whiteboard with names crossed out. Two silhouettes at the table.
On-screen: "Early 1991: the hunt begins"

> In early 1991, a small team inside the CIA was given a single job: find the cause. Among them were two longtime officers, Sandy Grimes and Jeanne Vertefeuille. They went back through the failed cases, one at a time, looking for what they had in common.
>
> It was slow, frustrating work. For a long time, it led nowhere.
>
> And then, they looked at something no one had wanted to look at. They looked at the money.

## 6. The money

Visual: a bank statement graphic with deposits highlighted; a house exterior; a luxury car (illustration). Whip-cut between them.
On-screen: "August 1992" then "$600,000+ on a house and cars"

> In August 1992, Sandy Grimes found a pattern. Ames had made large bank deposits right after meetings with a particular Soviet official. **[V]**
>
> Then came the lifestyle. By 1993, on a government salary, Ames had spent more than six hundred thousand dollars on a new house and luxury cars. The numbers did not add up.
>
> The case moved to the FBI. In May 1993, the bureau opened its investigation. And the hunt for the mole had finally become a hunt for a man.

## 7. Ten months of surveillance

Visual: surveillance-style framing (illustration): a figure at a mailbox, a street at night, a city skyline (Bogotá) with a map pin.
On-screen: "Oct 13, 1993: chalk mark" then "Nov 1, 1993: Bogotá, Colombia"

> For ten months, FBI agents and investigative specialists watched him. Physical surveillance. Electronic surveillance. Every move, every routine.
>
> On October 13, 1993, they saw him make a chalk mark on a mailbox. It was a signal. It told the Russians he intended to meet them in Bogotá, Colombia.
>
> On November 1, agents watched him in Bogotá. And separately, they saw his Russian handler.
>
> There was more. Investigators found incriminating evidence in his home, and on his home computer. The case was no longer a suspicion. It was proof.

## 8. The arrest

Visual: a quiet residential street at dawn (illustration); a courtroom sketch style frame.
On-screen: "Feb 21, 1994: arrested, Arlington, Virginia" then "Apr 28, 1994: guilty plea" then "Life, no parole"

> On February 21, 1994, the FBI arrested Aldrich Ames in Arlington, Virginia. They arrested his wife, Rosario, at the same time. She had helped him.
>
> Both pleaded guilty on April 28th. Ames was sentenced to life in prison, with no possibility of parole. Rosario received a sentence of sixty-three months.

## 9. How he got away with it

Visual: a Senate hearing-style illustration; a document page with the phrase "gross negligence" highlighted.
On-screen: "Senate Intelligence Committee, Nov 1, 1994"

> So how did a traitor stay hidden for nine years?
>
> The Senate Intelligence Committee gave its answer in November 1994. It found that the CIA had been guilty of, quote, "gross negligence, both individually and institutionally," in creating and allowing the environment in which Ames could operate without being caught.
>
> The agency was watching the world for spies. It was not watching the people inside the building.

## 10. Close

Visual: a closed file folder; fade to a silhouette of a second figure, with a shadowed FBI badge shape (illustration).
On-screen: "Next: the FBI's own traitor"

> Ames was not the last traitor inside American intelligence. A few years later, the FBI had to face its own. His name was Robert Hanssen. And that story is next.
